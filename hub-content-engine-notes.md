# Games as content engines — notes

> From a conversation (late 2026) about why games are the only product type
> with a natural content engine, the Megabonk case study, and what this means
> for the user's portfolio and distribution strategy.

---

## 1. The insight

Games generate content as a byproduct of development AND gameplay. Every update
is a devlog. Every new weapon is a video. Every bug fix is a story. Every player
interaction is potential viral content. The game *is* the content engine.

SaaS products, tools, and utilities don't generate content. "I added a new chart
to the stats page" is not a video. "I fixed a bug in the Supabase sync" is not
a devlog. The product is the product. There's nothing new to say unless you add
features, and after 10 features you've said everything.

This is the structural difference: **games have infinite content surfaces. SaaS
products have finite ones.**

## 2. Why this fits the user

1. **Already has game dev experience.** RocketMastery, MITE, FPS_Test, FPSWebTest,
   GameTest. Understands movement, weapons, projectiles, netcode, targets,
   spawning, scoring. Not a new domain.

2. **FPSWebTest already has a content engine.** The "robustify round" dev loop
   produces reports, test plans, netcode deep dives. Each round is a piece of
   content. The game generates content as a byproduct of development. You just
   haven't published it.

3. **Unique angle.** "I'm building a browser FPS with server-authoritative
   netcode, lag comp with rewind, and autopilot bots — and I'm directing AI to
   do it." That's a story people want to follow. The "AI-directed game dev"
   angle is novel and interesting.

4. **Incremental pursuit fits.** You don't need to "launch" a game. You need to
   **publish one devlog per week.** "Today I added a new weapon." "Today I fixed
   the lag comp." "Today I added a new map." Each is content. Each drives
   discovery. The game grows as the content grows.

## 3. The Megabonk case study

Megabonk sold **1 million copies in two weeks** at ~$10/unit, netting ~$7M.
Made by one person (vedinad) in 11 months. Launched in the most crowded period
of 2025 (Hades 2, Hollow Knight Silksong). No paid promo. No big budget.

### The marketing playbook (engineered virality)

1. **The name** sparked 2M+ trailer views and X threads ("Put shades on a
   skateboarding skeleton, sell a million copies").
2. **Self-deprecating humor** in dev posts ("Gonna go bonk my mega now" after
   100K sales).
3. **TikTok/YouTube Shorts hooks** baked into the game — replay export, meme
   templates, absurd bonk combos.
4. **Streamer seeding** — keys to mid-tier streamers (5K-50K followers) via X
   DMs. Tracked via Twitch metrics.
5. **Community-driven content** — players generated memes, clips, discussions.
   The game *was* the content engine.
6. **"Anti-AAA" positioning** — short, repayable, funny. Thrived by being the
   opposite of big-budget games.
7. **Tongue-in-cheek launch video** — announced debut during the Silksong/Hades 2
   launch window with a video mocking the crowded period.
8. **Player-generated content** — replay export + share buttons early.
   Crowdsource hype with player-generated content.

### Key metrics
- 1M+ copies in 2 weeks
- ~$7M net (after Valve's 30% cut)
- 11 months development
- No paid promo
- 90% of indies flop due to poor initial engagement
- Post-peak, players dropped 60% in week 3 (virality is fleeting)

### The broader trend
- October's AAA flood (ARC Raiders) crowds visibility, but Megabonk thrived by
  being "anti-AAA."
- Indies with 1-10 devs make up 70% of Steam sales.
- 2025 saw 15% more indies, but only 5% sustain >100K sales.
- Diversify: export to Epic/Game Pass for 10-15% uplift.

## 4. Why this is different from SaaS

| | SaaS (BellRack) | Game (FPSWebTest) |
|---|---|---|
| Content generation | Manual (you write posts) | Automatic (dev + gameplay generate content) |
| Discovery channel | SEO, community, word of mouth | YouTube/TikTok algorithm, streamers, player clips |
| Content surface | Finite (10 features = 10 posts) | Infinite (each update, weapon, map, bug = content) |
| Compounding? | No (one-shot posts) | Yes (consistent devlogs compound) |
| Requires ongoing production? | Yes (you write everything) | No (game generates content, you document) |
| Ceiling | $4k/mo (500 users) | $50-$200/mo (small game market) |
| Time to first user | 1-3 months (community) | 3-6 months (content compounding) |
| Risk | Low (predictable) | Medium (viral or small) |

The key insight: **games have a natural content engine. SaaS products don't.**
You don't need to "make content" for a game - you need to **document what you're
already doing.** The development process IS the content.

## 5. The honest caveat

Games are also the **most competitive** market. The browser FPS space is small
(Krunker fading, Narrow.One dead, Shell Shockers aging). The "content engine"
works, but it works in a small pond. You're not going to get 1M views on a
devlog about a browser FPS. You might get 1,000. And 1,000 views doesn't make
money.

But the content engine doesn't need to make money directly. It needs to:
1. Build an audience (even small).
2. Drive discovery (people find the game through the content).
3. Create a feedback loop (players tell you what to build next).
4. Generate the "one by one" subscribers (people who follow the devlog and try
   the game).

The content engine is the **top of the funnel.** The game is the **product.** The
funnel is free. The product is where the money is (if any).

## 6. Devlogs vs. short-form (the Megabonk distinction)

Megabonk's marketing was **not devlogs**. Devlogs are "I built X today, here's
how" (educational, technical). Megabonk's playbook was different:

1. **The game was inherently memeable.** The name, the humor, the absurd physics.
   The game *itself* generated shareable moments. Players made clips. The game
   was the content engine, not the dev.
2. **TikTok/YouTube Shorts hooks baked into gameplay.** Replay export, meme
   templates, absurd bonk combos. The game produces clips that players want to
   share.
3. **Self-deprecating dev posts** (not devlogs). "Gonna go bonk my mega now"
   after 100K sales. Personality-driven, not technical.
4. **Streamer seeding.** Keys to mid-tier streamers (5K-50K followers) via X DMs.
   Tracked via Twitch metrics.
5. **Community-driven content.** Players generated memes, clips, discussions.
   The game *was* the content engine.

The distinction: **devlogs are "here's what I built." Megabonk's approach was
"look at this absurd thing that happened in my game."** The first is educational.
The second is entertainment. Both are content engines, but they work differently.

For FPSWebTest, the Megabonk playbook would be:
- Bake in shareable moments (funny bot behavior, absurd physics, impressive
  shots).
- Replay export so players can make clips.
- Self-deprecating dev posts on X/TikTok/Shorts ("the bots just outsmarted me
  again").
- Streamer seeding (keys to mid-tier streamers).
- Let players generate the content. The game is the engine.

Devlogs are a **supplement** to this, not the core strategy. The core strategy is:
**make the game inherently shareable, and let players generate the content.**

## 7. Short-form is the compounding channel (not devlogs)

The "publish 100 pieces" test works for short-form, not for devlogs:

- **Short-form (15-60s)**: easy to produce (record 30s of gameplay, add text
  overlay, post). Can produce 3-5 per week. Compounds through the algorithm.
  Megabonk's YouTube Shorts generated 20M+ views.
- **Devlogs (5-15 min)**: harder to produce (need to explain, show code, narrate).
  Can produce 1 per week max. Compounds through search, but slower.
- **The key difference**: short-form is about **shareable moments** (entertainment).
  Devlogs are about **technical depth** (education). Short-form has a larger
  audience. Devlogs have a smaller, more engaged audience.

The "publish 100 pieces" test:
- 100 short-form clips (15-30s each) = 3-5 per week for 5-7 months. Realistic.
  Compounds through TikTok/Shorts/Reels algorithm.
- 100 devlogs (5-15 min each) = 1 per week for 2 years. Less realistic.
  Compounds through YouTube search, but slower.

**Short-form is the compounding channel. Devlogs are the supplement.**

From the research:
- "YouTube Shorts > TikTok for system-heavy or static games" (gamedev Reddit).
- "Shorts is the one where a clear, searchable title keeps paying off long after
  you post" (treat it more like a title-first channel).
- "Post 3 times a week" is the recommended cadence for short-form.
- "Repurpose TikToks and Reels for Shorts to maximize ROI."

## 8. What I'd actually do

1. **Pick FPSWebTest** (your 5-year dream, already has a robustify dev loop,
   already has 100+ reports).
2. **Bake in shareable moments.** Replay export, funny physics, absurd bot
   behavior. The game should generate clips that players want to share. This is
   the Megabonk playbook - the game IS the content engine.
3. **Record 3-5 short clips per week** (15-30s each). Gameplay moments, not
   devlogs. "Look at this absurd thing that happened." Post to TikTok, Shorts,
   Reels.
4. **Self-deprecating dev posts** (not devlogs). "The bots just outsmarted me
   again." Personality-driven, not technical. X/TikTok/Shorts.
5. **Seed to mid-tier streamers.** X DMs to 5K-50K follower streamers. Track via
   Twitch metrics.
6. **Publish devlogs as a supplement.** "Today I added X. Here's how." 5 minutes
   of screen recording + text overlay. Not polished. Authentic. But this is the
   supplement, not the core strategy.
7. **Let players generate the content.** Replay export, share buttons, meme
   templates. The game produces clips. Players share them. The clips drive more
   discovery.

No one-shot strategies. No "post on Reddit and hope." The core strategy is:
make the game inherently shareable, and let players generate the content.
Short-form clips are the compounding channel. Devlogs are the supplement.
