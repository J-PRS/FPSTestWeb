## The libraries worth knowing

**Yuka** (`mugen87/yuka`) — the closest thing to a full game-AI lib in JS. Standalone engine-agnostic: steering behaviors (seek/flee/pursuit/evade/wander/obstacle-avoid, blended via a manager), goal-driven agents, FSM states, perception/senses, plus a navmesh implementation and graph pathfinding. Basically "what you'd hand-roll, done properly." If you want *structure* for the bot brain — you already have `shared/botBrain.ts` utility AI — Yuka is where the steering + goal arbitration could come from.

**recast-navigation-js** — WASM port of Recast/Detour, the industry-standard navmesh toolkit. Generates navmeshes from geometry at runtime, Detour path queries, crowd sim, three.js helpers (`@recast-navigation/three` generates a navmesh straight from `THREE.Mesh`es). This is the "real" answer to nav.

**navcat** (same author) — pure-JS reimplementation of the same algorithms, JSON-serializable navmeshes, no WASM. Lighter, newer, less battle-tested.

**three-pathfinding** (donmccurdy) — query-only: A* + clampStep over a navmesh you supply as geometry. Doesn't *build* navmeshes — you need Blender/Recast to make one.

## The catch — Tribes-shaped problem

Navmesh libs assume floor-walking agents. Tribes movement is skiing + jets — your bots won't *play* Tribes on a navmesh; they'd ground-walk like Quake bots. That's actually how the real games did it: Tribes 2 bots ran on waypoint graphs + LOS checks, not navmeshes, and ski-speed traversal was fake/teleport-ish.

And your maps are **procedural heightfields + yaw-OBB structures**, not arbitrary mesh levels — you'd be rasterizing a heightfield into triangles just to feed Recast. A coarse **walkability grid + A\*** over the heightfield is a much better fit: cells blocked by slope threshold or structure footprint, your `maps/*.json` specs give you flag/base/pad positions as strategic nodes for free. ~200 lines, no dependency, deterministic across client/server — same reason `shared/movement.ts` exists.

You're right, and the research backs you up — the technique you're describing exists and has a name: **context steering** (Andrew Fray, *"Context Steering: Behavior-Driven Steering at the Macro Scale"*). It's exactly the "primitive senses → organic motion" model. And your terrain makes it *easier* than mesh levels, not harder.

## How context steering works

Instead of computing *a* direction, the bot scores **all** directions — 16–24 slots around a circle — using two maps:

- **Interest map**: how much do I want to go each way? (toward nugget, toward flag, toward engagement range)
- **Danger map**: how bad is each way? (cliff, structure, enemy line of fire, other bots)

Danger *masks* interest — a direction you want that's also a cliff face gets zeroed, not subtracted. Then pick the best surviving slot, smooth, move. No paths, no grid, no stuck states — "can't go that way" just makes adjacent slots win instead. This is the fix for the exact failure modes you're seeing: moonwalking into hillsides, herds bumping in lockstep, hard-coded stuck-breaks.

## Your senses are nearly free

Heightfield terrain is a dream for this — senses are just **function calls**, no raycasts against meshes:

- **Slope sense**: `terrain.getHeight(x + dir*d, z + dir*d)` at ~3m per slot → uphill-climbing directions get danger proportional to grade. Costs ~24 getHeight calls per bot per think, and you already do similar for contour-slide.
- **Structure sense**: your structures are yaw-OBBs — "is this direction heading into the box" is a 2D dot check per structure within range. Cheap.
- **Threat sense**: danger slot bonus toward enemies that can see the bot / away from aim lines. Instant strafing-orbits for free.
- **Separation**: repulsion slots toward nearby bots — the herd-spreading you were faking with random stuck-breaks falls out naturally.

## The split that works

Keep the layered structure — this is the consensus pattern from the BT/utility-AI literature:

- **Utility layer** (what you have in `shared/botBrain.ts`) decides *what I want right now* — collect nugget vs fight vs flag vs roam, with hysteresis so it doesn't flicker.
- **Context steering** decides *how to move there* — turns the chosen goal into a smooth physical path that organically routes around terrain, other bots, and structures.
- **Whisker fallbacks stay**: context steering handles 95% of motion; keep a 2–3-ray "wall ahead" probe as a breaker for rare cases (box canyon, inside a structure's concave corner).

## Honest caveat

Context steering's known weakness is **local minima** — a bot at the base of a U-shaped valley with its goal behind the ridge will orbit the valley mouth forever. Terrain maps rarely produce that (heightfields are gentle), but flag-base placement inside a crater could trigger it. The cheap patch is a **memory bias**: slots toward recently-visited positions get slight danger, which acts like momentum away from explored dead ends — no pathing needed, and it preserves the organic feel.

**Verdict**: yes, skip A*. A context-steering mover (~150 lines over the heightfield) bolted under your existing utility brain gets you human-looking motion on organic terrain for a fraction of navmesh complexity — and it stays deterministic and shareable across client/server the same way `shared/movement.ts` is.
